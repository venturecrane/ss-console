# How an Engagement Goes

_A generic story drawn from our first Operator client. Every name and date is removed; the only figures kept are the proof figures from part 1. Read this in place of sitting in on a client call: this relationship runs almost entirely by email, so the email threads are the call._

## Summary

A small personal-injury litigation firm came to us wanting AI to take on its litigation drafting. In the first exchanges, the office manager sent a long list of drafting tasks. As the threads went on, the firm told us what it was really trying to get to: settled cases closed out properly, nothing slipping when a staff member is out, and attorneys getting first drafts in the firm's own format without rebuilding the file each time. We never ran a formal discovery phase. We sent back something concrete (a mapped workflow, a list of observations, a first draft on one of their real files), and the firm's reaction told us the objective. Each solution started on one real file, read by a person before anything moved. The firm then widened it one caseload at a time. Today attorneys email the Operator directly with one-line requests, and the firm decides what comes next.

## Annotated exchanges

**1. "Here is a wish list" becomes "here is the whole business."**
After the intro conversation, the office manager sent a long list of litigation tasks she hoped AI could handle. We did not reply with a proposal. We mapped one of them, how discovery moves through the firm, and asked where it was wrong. She corrected the details, then widened the request to the full life of a case and listed what slips most: tracking client signatures, deadlines after extensions, and records that keep arriving mid-case.
_What to notice (for the rep):_ a concrete artifact gets a better answer than a question does. People who can't name their objective in the abstract will name it the moment they correct a draft.

**2. "Clean up our tasks" becomes "the reason we called you."**
Once we were connected read-only, we sent observations: stale tasks, files with no assigned attorney, missing contact details. We asked how they wanted each one handled. She answered every item, then reframed the whole engagement. The real priority was getting settled cases fully closed out, work that mattered more to the firm than anything on the original list. She was clear about the split: we find, track and follow up, and her people negotiate and decide.
_What to notice (for the rep):_ listen for the pivot sentence, the "let me reframe this" moment. The first ask is often the visible symptom. The objective is often something the owner worries about and hasn't said out loud.

**3. "Can it do a chronology?" becomes "something we can actually use."**
The firm asked for a rush test. Two earlier vendors had produced documents far longer than the records they summarized. They pointed us at a past chronology they liked and asked us to match it on one live file. The feedback came in short, blunt notes: too long, and split the exhibits out. We fixed both on that same file. The approval came with a request to run the rest, and then a note that demand drafting was next.
_What to notice (for the rep):_ the objective was usability, not a chronology. Their own example, their own file and their own redlines decide what good looks like. Fast iteration earns the next ask.

**4. "How do I prompt it?" becomes "fit how I already work."**
The managing partner already used AI daily: he set up a project per case and uploaded the documents himself. He asked whether he should prompt the Operator the same way and whether he had to point it at files. We answered in his terms. Prompt it the same way, but skip the uploading, because it works like a paralegal who already has the file open. Earlier, when one of the Operator's replies used internal jargon, he forwarded it to us asking for help. We explained in plain words, owned the wording as our miss, and changed it.
_What to notice (for the rep):_ the decision-maker's objective is often "don't make me learn a new system." Meet existing habits, speak in their vocabulary, and treat every confusion as our defect, never theirs.

## Patterns to take into a pitch

- **Lead with where they're going, then show something.** Ask what the firm is trying to reach, then offer to map one workflow back to them. Their corrections are how the objectives come out.
- **Prove it on their file, in their format.** A first draft on a real matter, measured against an example they already like, persuades more than any demo.
- **Control first, autonomy earned.** Every routine starts by preparing work for a person. The client decides when anything runs on its own. Saying this early takes the most common worry off the table.
- **"Scratch that for now" is information, not a loss.** This firm paused a marketing agent and a demand format along the way, then came back to the marketing piece later on its own. Keep scope where they are.
- **Speak their language about money and effort.** The partner asked for usage explained in plain English and wanted the arrangement to be fair to both sides. Plain answers built more trust than polish.
- **Email is the meeting room.** For a busy practice, the conversation happens in threads they answer between depositions. Short, concrete, answerable messages keep it moving.
