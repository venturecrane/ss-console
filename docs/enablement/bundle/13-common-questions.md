# Common questions from business owners, with answers

These are the questions owners ask most, with the answer SMD gives. Each answer comes from SMD's own documents, named under it. Two rules apply to every answer: never name a price, a dollar amount or a fixed timeframe, and never promise a fit, a start date or a result. When a question goes beyond what is here, bring it to SMD at team@smd.services.

## "Is this just a chatbot?"

No. A chatbot waits for someone to type a question and answers it. The Operator has standing work it does on its own schedule whether or not anyone messages it: watching for what is slipping, sending the recurring update, moving the work along. When someone hands it a task, it carries it to done inside the business's own systems. A chatbot is a conversation; the Operator is a worker with a job.

Source: website Operator page, "How it compares" (file 02).

## "Another AI tool. I have watched people get burned by these."

It is not a tool you run; it is a worker SMD configures for your business and runs for you. Each Operator is configured for one business: its skills, its limits, its voice, its connections, its memory. Point to real work: for a personal injury law firm it has delivered 18 medical chronologies from more than 29,000 pages of records, every entry cited to the page it came from.

Sources: enablement kit part 4, objections (file 06); website case study (file 02).

## "Will it replace our people?"

No. It takes the connective busywork off the team, the follow-ups and handoffs that eat the day, so people spend their time on the work only they can do. The owner decides what the Operator handles and what stays with a person.

Source: website Operator page, "Will it replace our people?" (file 02).

## "How is this different from hiring someone?"

The honest comparison is against a hire. A new hire is capable and still spends months learning the business and its tools. The Operator learns the systems quickly, works across them, and what it learns stays with the business instead of leaving when a person does.

Source: website Operator page, "How is an Operator different from hiring someone?" (file 02).

## "Why not build our own AI agent?"

Some teams do. The cost is rarely the build; it is keeping it working: connecting it to the tools, watching it, fixing what drifts, and keeping up as the technology changes. With the Operator, the business gives feedback to a worker instead of taking on a second job operating one.

Sources: website Operator page, "Should we build our own AI agent" (file 02); product library, what-it-is.md, "Build it yourself, or have it built and run" (file 05).

## "My staff can just use an AI assistant."

They probably should. That makes a person faster at their own desk. It does not cover the seat when that person is out or leaves, and it does not do the standing work nobody asks for.

Source: enablement kit part 4, objections (file 06).

## "Can it do the work our business needs?"

Very likely, because the Operator is configured for each business: SMD writes its skills, connects it to the systems the business runs, and builds a connection or a skill where one is needed. Ask what the business is trying to get done, then describe what the Operator does for that work, in the present tense. The industry pages on the website show the seat it fills in each kind of business.

Sources: product library, what-it-is.md and built-versus-planned.md (file 05); website industry pages (file 03).

## "How do I know it won't do something on its own that embarrasses us?"

You write the limits. For every kind of action, you decide whether it acts on its own, asks first, drafts for a person to send, or may not do it at all, and anything not written down is refused. Messages to people outside your business can be set to arrive as drafts for your team to send. Some lines never move: it does not move money, and agreeing to something on your behalf or doing anything it cannot undo always waits for a person. It can never give itself more authority. Every action is checked against your limits in code before it runs.

Sources: How the Operator Works, views 3 and 4 (file 06); website Operator page (file 02); product library, permission-model.md (file 05).

## "Is my data safe? Who can see it?"

Each business gets its own isolated machine with its own encrypted storage, shared with no other client. Your files stay in your own systems; SMD does not keep copies. The Operator's mailbox sits in your own mail system. No person at SMD reads your files: they are handled only by AI agents, your own Operator and SMD's agents working in a workspace sealed to your business. SMD's console shows health, settings and the audit record of actions, never your business content, and SMD receives a notice for each staff request so it can see every request is answered. Your data is never used to train AI models, and what your Operator learns never benefits another client.

Sources: How the Operator Works, views 2, 6 and 7 (file 06); product library, isolation-and-data-custody.md (file 05); website security page (file 04).

## "Who owns what it learns?"

The business does. Its memory of how you work is a record kept on your own machine, and if the service ends it is exported to you along with the audit record.

Sources: website Operator page, "One Operator, one memory. All yours" (file 02); product library, memory.md and what-smd-commits-to.md (file 05).

## "What if it gets something wrong?"

AI output can be wrong, and the product is built so that your people review it before relying on it. Where the record does not support something, the Operator says so instead of guessing; where a check cannot complete, the action is refused. Big jobs go through an independent review before delivery. Responsibility follows the limits: the client is responsible for what the Operator does within limits the client wrote, and not for actions outside those limits or a failure by SMD to enforce them.

Source: product library, what-smd-commits-to.md, "AI output and the client's review" and "Responsibility and liability" (file 05).

## "How can I tell what it actually did?"

Every action is written to an audit record, one entry per action, each chained to the one before so a change would show. The Operator can read the record but cannot rewrite it. Your administrators read it in the Client portal, and you can hand an evidence packet to anyone, who can check it on SMD's public trust page without asking SMD.

Sources: How the Operator Works, view 7 (file 06); website Operator page and trust page (files 02 and 04).

## "How do we stop it if something goes wrong?"

Pause it from the Client portal; it stops all activity at once and stays off until someone turns it back on. You can also cut its access directly in your own systems.

Sources: How the Operator Works, "What managed means" (file 06); product library, client-controls.md and permission-model.md (file 05).

## "Will my team actually use it?"

There is nothing new to log in to. Staff email it like a colleague and get the work back in the same thread. Adoption is SMD's job: SMD sets it up, turns it on one area at a time, and keeps it working.

Sources: enablement kit part 4, objections (file 06); training curriculum, quiz question 3 (file 07).

## "What does it cost?"

A representative does not quote a price. Pricing is set by SMD for each engagement, compared to the cost of a hire rather than a software subscription, and SMD walks through it before any setup begins. On the website: a flat monthly subscription, scoped to the role built together, with an allowance for the heavy work set in the agreement, no hourly billing, and nothing on an invoice the client did not agree to first.

Sources: website Operator page, "It starts with an assessment" (file 02); product library, who-smd-sells-to.md (file 05); training tone rules (file 07).

## "How long does it take to set up?"

SMD does not publish timeframes; timing is agreed per engagement. Onboarding moves through phases (prepare, explore, realize, deploy, run), and each phase ends on proof, not on a date. Billing starts only after SMD states in writing that testing is complete and the client starts the subscription.

Source: product library, managed-service.md, "Onboarding phases" (file 05).

## "Who else is running this?"

Be straight: SMD is early and selective. A personal injury law firm runs an Operator today, and the website case study shows its work. For another kind of business, SMD configures the Operator for that business and builds its industry-specific skills with the first client in that industry. Offer a scoped start and show the real work.

Sources: enablement kit part 4, objections (file 06); product library, what-it-is.md, "Industry packs", and built-versus-planned.md (file 05).

## "Do you have SOC 2? What uptime do you guarantee?"

SMD does not hold SOC 2 or ISO 27001 of its own and says so; its main sub-processors are independently SOC 2 audited. It does not publish an uptime percentage or offer service credits. Its commitment is about detecting problems, responding by severity, and communicating honestly, and the standard agreement sets how quickly a security incident affecting client data is reported. It carries technology errors and omissions and cyber coverage, including coverage written for AI.

Sources: product library, what-smd-commits-to.md (file 05); website security page (file 04); How the Operator Works, "Standard terms" (file 06).

## "If it doesn't work out, how do we get out?"

The client can cancel in the Client portal. On exit, SMD delivers the audit record and the Operator's memory in exportable form, destroys the dedicated machine and storage, and confirms destruction in writing, within the periods the standard agreement sets. Everything the Operator wrote into the client's own systems is already there.

Source: product library, what-smd-commits-to.md, "Ending the service, return and deletion" (file 05).

## "Do we need an Operator at all?"

Maybe not, and SMD will say so. Sometimes the right answer is a cleaner process, the right tool chosen well, or one integration that makes two systems talk. The assessment is how that gets decided, and the owner leaves with a clear recommendation either way.

Source: website home page, "Not every problem needs an Operator" (file 02).
